Two maps of
becoming

Development moves in two ways at once. Cells travel through the body, and cell states travel through what a cell is expressing. Zebrahub watched both — but not in the same animal.

10 hours – 10 days post fertilisation
after Lange et al. 2024  ·  Zebrahub, CZ Biohub

The two pages beside this one each answer half a question. The Wang map follows nuclei through a microscope and can say that this cell came from that one, but never what either was expressing. The DanioCell map reads what half a million cells were expressing, but each of them only once, so it can never say where any of them went. Zebrahub did both experiments.

On one side, transcriptomes. What makes them unusual is not the number but the bookkeeping: the embryos were dissociated one at a time, so every cell still knows which of individual fish it came from — four fish at each of stages. That turns a question you cannot normally ask into an easy one. How alike are two animals of the same age?

On the other, a living embryo under a light sheet: nuclei followed across frames, through divisions. Here the lineage is real. A cell is watched dividing, and both daughters are watched afterwards.

And the thing to hold on to: these are not the same cells. The transcriptomes come from forty embryos that were taken apart to be read. The tracks come from one other embryo that stayed alive and was never sequenced. Nothing on this page registers one to the other, because nothing can. They are two maps of the same process, not two views of the same specimen.
