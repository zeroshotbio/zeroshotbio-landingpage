24–48 hpf: the inferred skeleton

One hundred and eighty-six cell states, one hundred and seventy-three arrows between them, and a published verdict on almost every arrow. Nothing on this page watched a cell divide.

Trapnell lab reference v2.2.1

 

A fate map is a claim about where cells go. This one is assembled from transcriptional similarity over time — states that resemble each other at adjacent hours are joined by an arrow — and that is a fundamentally different kind of evidence from watching a nucleus split in two. The corpus behind this page contains no observation of a division between 24 and 48 hpf. Every arrow here is a hypothesis.

What makes the graph worth drawing anyway is that its authors adjudicated it. Each edge was checked against the published literature and given a verdict, and a quarter of them did not survive. Drawing the graph without those verdicts would make it look far more settled than it is; drawing it with them turns a skeleton into an argument you can inspect.

These are inferred developmental transitions, not observed lineage. No cell on this page was tracked. Where an arrow is drawn, it means two transcriptional states resemble each other across time — not that one was seen to become the other.

The horizontal axis is depth in the graph, not time. Depth is a property of the structure, so every arrow points rightward and 173 of them stay legible without a physics simulation. Time is carried separately, on each state, as a six-point wild-type abundance trajectory — and it corroborates the structure rather than defining it: of the edges where both ends are timed, most run from an earlier-peaking state to a later-peaking one.
